# Chapter 2 writing plan · The Architecture of the Internet

Go back to the path from the campus library to the video server in Chapter 1. Ask what it would take to replace one link between routers while the laptop and the server carry on as before. Give the reader a reason to try an unusual link. Cooville has a working local network, Internet access is available across the valley in Buffering Heights, and the terrain rules out wire and radio between the villages. Introduce Returno as the resident trying to connect Cooville and Homer as the neighbor willing to share his connection. Explain how a bird trained to fly home to Homer could carry data across the gap. Let the reader decide what to send on it before naming any layers. By the end, they should be able to explain which machines need to know how the replacement link works.

1. **Get one message across the valley.** Draw the homes of the two flocks, the direction each can fly and the morning walk that makes tomorrow's flights possible. Give the reader only the constraints needed for this first decision. Introduce the 15-minute flight and the 50-bird daily budget per direction when a second customer turns up.
2. **Decide who gets the next departure.** Recall the private-wire problem from Chapter 1 with a small drawing of dedicated links versus a switch. On a conventional link, show a quiet sender's reserved slot and a waiting sender who could have used it. Compare reserved service and packet switching on the same little timeline. Return to the birds and let a late urgent message break an otherwise sensible batching rule. Distinguish waiting for departure from travel time.
3. **Send the message beyond the coop.** Follow one packet from an ordinary Cooville computer through the two village routers to a server. What must survive each handoff? Introduce the destination IP address, the next hop and the best-effort datagram as answers. Use the same path to explain return routing. Ask what a lost bird or nightfall does to the promise.
4. **Deliver it to the right program.** Give the server two programs and one incoming message. Walk the same message down the sender's layers, across the change of link, and up the receiver's layers. Draw which header is replaced at the village router and which identifiers carry on. Explain ports, UDP messages and TCP's ordered byte stream as solutions to specific application needs. Keep the stack small enough to redraw from memory.
5. **Find out whether the whole file arrived.** Let the far coop acknowledge delivery, then let the next link lose the data. Who could tell the sender that the application got its file? Derive the end-to-end principle and show where local recovery can still help. Contrast a file ready to send at dawn with an application that waits for a reply before every next step. Ask what still works, what performs badly and what might time out.
6. **Hand FeatherNet back to its designers.** Revisit the four original Wingternet decisions with a compact design brief, one counterexample and a chance to revise the first sketch. Leave the mechanics of obtaining addresses and finding routes for later chapters.

Use Chapter 1's conversational register and the lecture's dry humor. Introduce each section with the trouble the previous design has just bought us. Tie each new term to something the reader has already pictured. Keep source citations and distinctions between class material, adapted questions and invented traces in concise chapter notes. Check the manuscript, diagrams and exercises against Lectures 1, 2 and 4 and Quiz 1 (2025), pp. 1–4. Build the book and verify the reading order and links.
