The avian RFC is dated April 1, 1990 and proposes printing, attaching with duct tape, and scanning. It is an experimental RFC. [RFC 1149](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc1149)

The file check and distinctions between local recovery, end-to-end acknowledgment, and application success follow Saltzer, Reed, and Clark's examples of careful file transfer and delivery acknowledgment. The case in which Homer acknowledges a packet that is later discarded is original. [End-to-End Arguments in System Design](https://web.mit.edu/saltzer/www/publications/endtoend/endtoend.pdf)

A concrete FeatherNet implementation would have to choose which IP versions it supports and meet their link-size requirements. For example, IPv6 requires a link to handle datagrams of at least 1,280 bytes, using link-specific splitting and reassembly if a single frame cannot hold that much. Figure 2.5 omits link trailers, byte lengths, and mutable IP fields. The chapter points out the decrement of the hop allowance. [RFC 8200, §5](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8200#section-5)

## Worked checks

- **Figure 2.1.** The B-class birds fly from Cooville to Buffering Heights and are carried back in the morning. The C-class flock does the reverse. Fifty per direction is the daily maximum with both flocks at full strength and replenished at their launch points.
- **Figures 2.2–2.3.** Four computers need 4 × 3 ÷ 2 = 6 private wires or four links to the switch. B2 starts at slot 3 with strict reservations and slot 2 with arrival-order packet switching. B3 starts at slot 5 and slot 3 respectively. The tie rule at time zero puts A1 first. The arrival at time 2 does not move B3 ahead of B2.
- **Figures 2.4–2.5.** Outbound datagrams reach the Cooville router, then the Buffering Heights router, then the server via the ordinary Internet. Destination routing for the reply must direct traffic to Buffering Heights, then Cooville, then the local host. UDP port 4001 is the weather listener. The reply addresses the original client on port 51000. In the model, the villages' routers need appropriate link support and forwarding entries. The server can keep the same UDP application.
- **Figure 2.6.** A sequential exchange takes 2 × 15 = 30 minutes over the valley and three take 90 minutes, excluding other links and any waiting or processing. The illustrative ordinary link gives 2 × 10 = 20 milliseconds and 60 milliseconds respectively. Parallel launches do not reduce the transit time of any one load. They make it possible to deliver multiple loads in that time, subject to the daily bird budget and capacity per bird. No flash capacity or actual Internet-side latency is stipulated.
- **The final counterexample.** Immediate dispatch serves the routine load and leaves no flight if the urgent message appears. Waiting preserves that option and delays the routine load on either day. A rule that waits can avoid wasting the flight when no urgent message appears by dispatching the routine load by the last feasible departure. Priority, delivery time, and daily goodput can favor different choices.

## Open source questions

The quiz does not supply a staff answer or a telemetry trace for FeatherNet. Neither the media capacity in bytes nor a packet loss rate is specified. The chapter leaves both as policy inputs. Actual TCP survival would depend on implementation timers, application deadlines, and connectivity across the availability window. The worked example fixes the physical travel time and leaves those implementation choices open.
