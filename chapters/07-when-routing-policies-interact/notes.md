# Chapter 7 source notes

## Lecture argument

- [Lecture 8, lines 12581–12754](../../assets/f26-subtitles.txt#L12581) returns to convergence after Ethernet and shortest paths. The class distinguishes a policy that withdraws an offer on its own from a fixed rule that reacts to new advertisements. A student proposes that rejecting paths with repeated ASes might provide a measure of progress. The lecturer asks what quantity would change monotonically and discusses using failed hunches to refine a proof.
- [Lecture 8, lines 12758–12939](../../assets/f26-subtitles.txt#L12758) gives the three-route-choice counterexample with origin AS 0. The exact accepted paths matter. AS 3 rejects `[3, 1, 2, 0]` even though the path has no repeated AS. The lecture uses staggered advertisements starting with 3 choosing its detour via 1. The manuscript retains the rankings and starts with all direct routes, then uses update order 1, 3, 2 to match the new figure. The argument by exhausting AS 1's two choices is an added proof.
- [Lecture 8, lines 12842–12919](../../assets/f26-subtitles.txt#L12842) contains the questions about when to announce a changed path, whether a network can advertise one path while using another, and what a packet sees if its advertised path changes in flight. The text adapts the third question and describes ordinary destination-based forwarding. The classroom suggestion of withdrawing before switching still leaves propagation delays, so the text makes no delivery guarantee.
- [Lecture 8, lines 12941–13069](../../assets/f26-subtitles.txt#L12941) asks which displayed configurations are stable. Its reproduced reasoning reverses AS 1's ranking and settles 1, then 3, then 2. The missing slide and overlapping speech prevent reconstructing all three original answer options with confidence. The chapter adds the two-source example from [Griffin, Shepherd, and Wilfong, Fig. 3](https://www.cs.bu.edu/faculty/gacs/courses/cs535/papers/BGP-ToN-Griffin.pdf) to distinguish a stable assignment from convergence under all permitted schedules.
- [Lecture 8, lines 13082–13298](../../assets/f26-subtitles.txt#L13082) raises the global registry, then uses customer–provider and peer relationships, ranking by route class, and the acyclic hierarchy. The three-stage proof sketch is the chapter's single-prefix presentation of the sufficient condition. [Gao and Rexford, §§IV–V](https://courses.cs.duke.edu/fall24/compsci514/readings/stable-internet-routing.pdf) treat a fuller BGP model and provide weaker preference guidelines than the strict customer, peer, provider order used in class. The temporary export-rule violation and its log question are new.
- [Lecture 8, lines 13300–13315](../../assets/f26-subtitles.txt#L13300) calls trying successively disappearing alternatives “path hunting” and links it to the earlier “bad news travels slowly” discussion. The route-collector example and the distinction between repeating the whole simulation state and seeing a repeated local snapshot are new.
- [Lecture 8, lines 13453–13663](../../assets/f26-subtitles.txt#L13453) introduces the Facebook outage through the user-facing name lookup. The transcript misdates it as 2024 and suggests that a cached address would have restored access. [Facebook Engineering's October 2021 report](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/10/05/networking-traffic/outage-details/) dates the event to October 4, 2021, traces the DNS withdrawals to a health check following loss of backbone connectivity, and reports that the service itself lost connectivity. The manuscript's cache experiment deliberately isolates the DNS failure before the real incident, and its network-detective question is new.
- Lecture 8's Seattle–Austin handoff, prepending, and longest-prefix recap overlap earlier chapters. Its example of a single AS carrying a subset of its internal traffic through a carrier is at [lines 12366–12578](../../assets/f26-subtitles.txt#L12366). It remains source material for a possible exercise on exceptions to the ordinary loop check and on dividing a destination into prefixes.

## Incidents, assessments, and worked checks

- Lecture 7, lines 11474–11506, motivates the YouTube incident. Lecture 25, lines 40964–41044, revisits the use of probes from working and failing networks. The on-call question and test address are new. The two-origin diagram summarizes the original and unauthorized announcements visible before YouTube announced its own /24, with intermediate networks elided. The date, lengths, origins, and upstream are from [RIPE NCC's RIS case study](https://www.ripe.net/about-us/news/youtube-hijacking-a-ripe-ncc-ris-case-study/).
- The rendered [2024 Final, Q5–Q6, pp. 3–4](../../assets/cs356-final-2024.pdf#page=3) supplies the redrawn four-spoke graph, relationship rules, and paired design problems. In the adaptation, the schedule, the request to find a stable assignment under the flapping policies, the one-change challenge, and the worked solutions are new. The worked trace returns to DDDI with w next after twelve activations. DIDI and IDID are both fixed points. Ranking the direct route first at w forces the successive choices at z, y, and x. The outer ring violates the acyclic hierarchy assumption. Customer-first ranking in the exam leaves the provider-versus-peer ranking free.
- [Quiz 1, 2024, pp. 4–7](../../assets/cs356-quiz1-2024.pdf#page=4) was also inspected, including the six business-relationship diagrams used by Q5–Q10. The final's paired questions let the reader reuse one topology throughout.
- The simulator is a single-prefix, serial best-response model with instantaneous visibility of selected paths on activation. Its repeated-state check includes the next scheduled AS. The 48-run sweep starts all-direct. All six orders cycle with three detour-first rankings, and the other 42 runs settle. These runs illustrate the fixed-choice proof. They do not enumerate message delays or model BGP timers.
